Two weeks ago I wrote [a breakdown of Huang's resignation]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/13/nvidia-jensen-huang-resigns.html) concluding: "NVIDIA will survive this; the myth of the infallible founder won't." Half that forecast is already obsolete: **per media reports, Jensen Huang is coming back to NVIDIA**. Here's the full chronicle — from investigation to revenge — with honest labeling: what's documented and what's still only in the press.

## A note on method

At writing time, the primary sources on the return (NVIDIA press releases, the final investigation report) were unavailable to me, and part of the story is known from media reports and channels. All of that is marked **"per reports"**. The November 8–16 events are confirmed by NVIDIA's releases and reporting by CNBC, AFP, WSJ and Bloomberg — linked in my [previous breakdown]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/13/nvidia-jensen-huang-resigns.html).

## Act I. The fall (November 8–12)

Briefly, for those who missed it:

- **November 8** — the board opens an investigation; Huang publishes his confession ("I was unfaithful to my wife"); the stock drops 10% in two days.
- **November 10** — the independent directors retain Joon Kim (Wachtell, Lipton) for "a thorough and independent investigation".
- **November 12** — resignation, immediate and from all posts. Colette Kress — interim CEO. Alphabet had already booked a $1B charge for fines; Reuters reported a third affair; and the company's market cap had already shed roughly **$1.27 trillion** over the month.

It seemed final. The board made clear the decision was irreversible.

## Act II. The siege (November 13–17)

This is where it departed from every crisis-management textbook.

**The industry wouldn't let go.** An unexpectedly broad coalition stood up for Huang:

- an **open letter** of support gathered **over 156 thousand signatures**;
- among the signatories: **Tim Sweeney** (Epic Games) and **Elon Musk**;
- Musk, per November 12 insiders, had already **offered Huang a spot at his companies** — a "come to us if you don't return" option;
- the support group, per reports, reached **into lobbying the US government**.

**In parallel — an ideological campaign.** The media accused Huang of inflating the "AI bubble" (the money round-trip: NVIDIA pouring money into customer startups, the startups buying NVIDIA chips, the stock flying to space — see [my breakdown]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/11/16/meta-fine-huang-comeback.html)). The slander and the support strangely reinforced each other: the more mud, the more sympathizers.

**November 17 — the earnings report.** The first in 33 years without Huang. Kress promised "to continue the work that made NVIDIA a leader". Per reports, the report landed on plan — but the market was waiting not for numbers, for **news of a successor**. None came.

## Act III. The reversal (per reports)

And that's where the board's position cracked. The directors, who in mid-November were saying **"there will be no return"** — phrased as final — by month's end, per media reports, agreed to bring the founder back.

What could have turned the board? A reconstruction (each point is my interpretation, not a fact):

1. **The November 17 report had no succession strategy.** An interim CEO is not an answer for a company that lost a quarter of its cap.
2. **The industry's reputation cash.** When Sweeney and Musk are for you and anonymous accusations are against you, the board starts counting money, not morals.
3. **The investigation's outcome.** If Kim's review found nothing that damaged the company (recall: the allegations concerned private life, not embezzlement), the legal obstacle to a return disappears.
4. **Fear of the "Musk trip".** Huang leaving for xAI or OpenAI is a scenario where NVIDIA loses not just a CEO but its symbol — to a competitor.

## What it means (opinion)

**The founder cult defeating governance, in its purest form.** The board fired the man "immediately and from all posts" — and two weeks later agreed to bring him back. It's a precedent business schools will study: formally the board is supreme; in practice — until it first collides with an "irresistible" founder backed by Musk, Sweeney and 156 thousand signatures.

**The parallel that begs to be made: Steve Jobs, 1997.** He left Apple in 1985 after a board conflict, returned 12 years later — and built an empire. The precedent works against skeptics: returning exiled founders is sometimes the best thing that happens to a company. But there's a difference: Jobs was brought back when Apple was near bankruptcy and he'd already been an advisor for a year. Huang is being returned to a company that is "still NVIDIA" — and where the investigation may not even be closed.

**Open questions** I'm watching:

- under what title? CEO — or a "returning founder" with caveats (chairman? CTO?)?
- what about Colette Kress — a caretaker hero, or a new CEO-partner?
- will the Kim report ever be published — or was burying it part of the deal?
- and the main one: **what happens to the scandal's tail** — the "bubble", the third affair, the congressional Democrats' probe? A return doesn't cancel the questions; it postpones them.

The moral for anyone building a company: fame is an asset until the first scandal, then a liability, then an asset again — if enough people love you. Documenting succession in advance is still the sturdier path.
