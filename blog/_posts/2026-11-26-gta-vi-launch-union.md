Grand Theft Auto VI launched on November 19 — and earned **$3.79 billion** on its first day, selling 32.5 million preorders: "the biggest entertainment launch in history" (per Rockstar). Meanwhile, on November 26, while the world played, Rockstar staff voted on unionization — and **75% said "no"**. Two numbers from one week that say a lot about game development in 2026.

<!--more-->

## The economics of a miracle

First, the scale — it is genuinely historic:

- **$3.79B on day one** — a record for entertainment in any medium. For comparison: the highest-grossing movie of all time made about $2.9B *over its entire run*. GTA VI passed that mark in a day.
- **32.5M preorders** — roughly one in four PlayStation 5s on the planet.
- Thirteen years of waiting, leaks (the hacker Arion Kurtaj was sentenced this week — and immediately released), delays — and the game still "lived up to the hype" per the reviewers.

Sam Houser, Rockstar's president: "We are incredibly humbled by the response from players around the world." The developers who postponed vacations for years probably feel the same.

## The vote that ran through the launch

In parallel with the launch, **from November 12 through 26**, Rockstar held a vote on formal union recognition (an initiative of the IWGB — Independent Workers Union of Great Britain). At stake was a framework for collective negotiation: **pay, overtime, bonuses, leave, pensions** — and, separately spelled out, the **right to consultation over future changes including AI implementation**.

The result: **only 25% in favor**. No union — but the IWGB says "Rockstar workers in the union will continue organizing even without formal recognition".

## How did that happen (and what it means)

Your product just made $3.79 billion — so here are three hypotheses on why 75% said no:

1. **The economics of mood.** For years, gamedev unions grew amid layoffs and crisis. A record success is a strong counterargument: "a winning company pays in bonuses — why pick a fight?".
2. **Fear in nice wrapping.** A recognition vote isn't a strike, but it's the first step toward confrontation. Some staff likely preferred not to "switch to war mode" with their employer at the peak of its (and their) success.
3. **The AI clause.** Possibly the most interesting part: the agreement included a consultation right on AI implementation. Some saw protection in it; others saw a "career brake" in an industry where mastering AI tools is already skill number one. The vote showed: **gamedev is not monolithic on AI**.

What's next: 25% is not zero. It's a core that will stay (the IWGB says so directly). And GTA VI still has its PC release and years of content ahead — and every decision about AI tools will be a test: will the views of that 25% be heard without a formal mechanism?

The week's irony: the company managed to make a historic product — but couldn't convince a quarter of its own people that their future is protected. $3.79 billion doesn't buy everything.
